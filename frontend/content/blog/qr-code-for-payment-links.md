"Payment QR code" means two different things, and the wrong one on a counter costs you money in the most literal way. One kind is a link: scan it with a camera, a checkout page opens, the customer pays there. The other kind is a payload your bank app reads directly: scan it in the payment app and money moves. They look identical printed, they are not interchangeable, and only one of them is a thing QRly makes.

## Two things called a payment QR code

| | Payment link QR code | Native payment QR code |
|---|---|---|
| What the code contains | A URL: `paypal.me/...`, `buy.stripe.com/...`, `rzp.io/...` | A structured payload: `upi://pay?pa=...`, an EMVCo string, an EPC/SEPA block |
| What scans it | Any phone camera | A payment app or bank app |
| Where the money is taken | On the provider's web checkout | Inside the payer's own app |
| Who makes it | Anyone with a link, any QR generator | The payment app, the bank, or the acquirer |
| Can it be dynamic | Yes, it is a link | No, the app expects the payload itself |
| QRly makes it | Yes | No |

Native standards, briefly, because you will meet them: **UPI** in India, where the code is a `upi://pay` URI carrying the payee's address, name and optionally an amount, read by any UPI app. **EMVCo merchant-presented QR**, the format behind PayNow in Singapore, PromptPay in Thailand, PIX in Brazil and a number of other national schemes, read by participating bank apps. **EPC QR**, also called GiroCode, a block of text with an IBAN and amount that European banking apps read to pre-fill a SEPA transfer.

Those are static payloads. The bank app reads the text and acts on it; there is no web page and no redirect. Each of them is generated by the relevant app or bank — the "receive" or "my QR" screen in a UPI app, your acquirer's merchant portal, your banking app — and that is where they should come from, because a wrong character sends money to the wrong place. QRly generates URL short links only, so a QRly code cannot be a UPI or EMVCo or EPC code.

## How a payment link QR code works

A payment link is a URL from your provider that opens a hosted checkout. The common ones:

- **PayPal.me**: `paypal.me/yourname`, or `paypal.me/yourname/25` to preset an amount in your account currency. The payer sees your name and pays with their PayPal balance or card.
- **Stripe Payment Links**: made in the Stripe dashboard for a product or a fixed amount, with a `buy.stripe.com` URL. Stripe's dashboard will also draw a QR code of the link for you; that code encodes the same URL, statically.
- **Razorpay Payment Links and Payment Pages**: a link per invoice or a standing page, with an `rzp.io` or `pages.razorpay.com` URL, supporting UPI, cards and net banking on the checkout.

The customer's camera opens the link in the browser, the provider's checkout page loads, and the provider takes the payment. The QR code does nothing with money. It is a link to a page that does.

## When a dynamic code is the better choice

Encode the payment link directly with a static generator and it works, for that link, forever. That is fine for a one-off invoice. It is the wrong choice for anything printed and left out, for reasons that all come down to the link changing:

- **The amount changes.** A `paypal.me/name/25` link on a menu is wrong the day the price goes to 27. A Stripe Payment Link has its price set at creation; changing the price means a new link.
- **The product changes.** The same table card sells the spring special and then the summer one.
- **The provider changes.** You move from PayPal to Stripe, or add Razorpay for Indian customers. The code on five hundred flyers cannot move with you unless it is a short link.
- **The offer ends.** A fundraising drive, a pre-order window, an early-bird price.

With a [dynamic code](/blog/what-is-a-dynamic-qr-code) the printed code encodes `qrly.lol/<slug>`, the redirect sends the scanner on to the payment link with a 302, and you change which payment link from the dashboard. The change reaches every edge in under a minute. Set an [expiry date](/blog/qr-code-with-expiration-date) if the offer has an end, and after that date scans reach a plain expired page rather than a live checkout for a price you no longer honour.

For donations this is the whole game: one code on the collection box, the leaflet and the pew sheet, pointing this quarter at the roof appeal and next quarter at the food bank. The [donations post](/blog/qr-code-for-donations-and-nonprofits) goes further.

Setting it up on QRly:

1. Make the payment link in your provider's dashboard.
2. Paste it at [/create](/create) or on the [home page](/); no account is needed for this.
3. Choose an ending that says what it is: `qrly.lol/pay-the-cafe`. It is permanent once saved, because it is about to be printed.
4. Sign up to claim the link, so the destination is editable later.

The destination is checked against Google Safe Browsing when saved and re-checked weekly; provider checkout domains pass.

## The trust problem, and what helps

Payment codes are the favourite target of the sticker attack: someone sticks their own code over yours on the parking meter or the table, and the customer's money goes to them. The [quishing post](/blog/qr-code-phishing-quishing) covers the general problem; for payments, a few things reduce it.

**Show the domain.** A person who has been told to check where a code goes sees `qrly.lol/pay-the-cafe` in the camera preview before tapping. Better, with a [custom domain](/blog/custom-domain-qr-code), they see `pay.thecafe.com/table`. A code on your own hostname is one a customer can match to the name over the door, and it is one you can move to any provider by editing the destination. Custom domains need an account and one CNAME record.

**Say what the checkout will be.** "Pay by card via Stripe" or "Donate via PayPal" next to the code, so the customer knows which page to expect and notices if a different one appears.

**Make it hard to sticker.** Print the code as part of the menu or the sign rather than as a separate label, behind the counter glass rather than on the outside of it. Check it now and then.

**Keep the checkout with the provider.** Never point the code at a page you built that asks for card details; a customer who sees a Stripe or PayPal page has a reason to trust it that a home-made form does not.

## Print and placement

A payment code is scanned from close range by someone who is about to type a card number, so it needs to work first time. Two centimetres square on a table card or an invoice, 5 cm or more on a counter sign, dark on light, with a clear border of at least four modules, exported as SVG. The short link keeps the code sparse; a Stripe or Razorpay URL with its full path makes a dense one.

On an invoice, a static code encoding that invoice's payment link directly is entirely reasonable: the invoice is a one-off. Use a dynamic code wherever the same print is used more than once.

## What you learn from the scans

A dynamic payment code reports scans, not payments; the provider reports payments. Together they give you a conversion number: scans of the counter code against payments on that link. QRly records for each link total scans, unique visitors per day, hour and weekday in the scanner's local time, approximate city, device and OS, and the referrer, where none means a camera scan. Nothing runs on the customer's phone, no cookie is set, and the IP address is discarded once the geo fields are derived; the [privacy page](/privacy) lists every field.

## Frequently asked

**Can QRly make a UPI, EMVCo or SEPA payment QR code?**
No. Those are static payloads read by payment apps, not URLs, and they should come from your payment app, bank or acquirer. QRly makes URL codes, which is what a PayPal, Stripe or Razorpay payment link needs.

**How do I make a QR code for a PayPal.me or Stripe payment link?**
Copy the link from the provider, paste it at QRly's create page, and download the code. Make it dynamic if the print will be reused, so the amount or provider can be changed later without reprinting.

**Can I change the amount after the code is printed?**
With a dynamic code, yes: make a new payment link with the new amount and point the code's destination at it from the dashboard. A static code encoding the old link directly cannot be changed.

**Is a payment link QR code safe?**
As safe as the link. Keep the checkout on the provider's hosted page, show the domain and the provider's name next to the code, and consider a custom domain so the code carries your hostname. Check printed codes occasionally for stickers placed over them.

**Does a QRly payment code take a cut of the payment?**
No. The code is a link to your provider's checkout and QRly never sees the transaction. There is no paid plan and no per-scan charge; the cost page shows how the service runs at no cost.
